So here we looked at the **bhāṣyam** for the mantra 2.4.5, and we started looking at the **vārtika** — same ideas, but with a lot more elaboration. The section we were looking at last time is, why did **yājñavalkya** begin with, you know, a wife is not dear for the wife's sake. Why this statement? And the statements are meant to generate objectivity — generate **vairāgyam**. **vairāgyam** is objectivity. Because we are not trying to look at anything in a way which it is not. So we don't — see, we are very reasonable. We don't say anything is useless. But we say, we look at everything for what it really has. Anything — what it has, you see it as it has it, then it is objectivity. If you see something more or something less, that is your extra value addition. And wherever you love something, that love is extra value addition — because there is nothing in that object, that attribute called love or dearness. **priyatvam** is not in any object. This is hard to understand or accept. Because the only things we love are things other than ourselves, we think. And therefore, if I love all these things, and these things indeed are lovable, then we better love them. But if they are not lovable, we better know that. That's the whole idea here. Whatever we are going after, does it really have what I am going after? Am I really going after things for the sake of things? Or is my interest elsewhere? What do I want, really, in and through all my pursuits? And what's being said through these sentences is that what you really love is the pleased self. Pleased self — that "pleased," that extra, I'm adding, only because we think that it is the pleased self. **upaniṣad** does not do that. It just says, **ātmanastu** **kāmāya** — **na** **tu** **priya** **ātmanastu** **kāmāya**; Just **ātmanastu** **kāmāya**. So **ātmā** is dear. Everything else really does not have dearness in it.

If you love to look at the mirror, what do you really love? The mirror? Suppose the mirror shows you a face which is not very pleasant — you won't like to look at it too much, or you will try to do something to it. But even when you look at your face in the mirror, you suddenly find that, oh, I forgot my **tilakam**. Where do you put the **tilakam**? On the mirror? Where do you put it? You put it here, and then it shows up there. Why? Why? Because you know that what you are looking at is your face. You are not looking at the mirror for the sake of the mirror. The mirror does not have anything beautiful about it. The mirror may be beautiful in its own way. But you are not looking at the mirror to look at the mirror. You want to see your beautiful face. And how many ever times you see it in a day, it's not enough. It always is nice to look at it. So when you look at it, you don't beautify that face — the face in the mirror. The mirror is not a necessity for your face to be there. The mirror cannot show you anything other than the face. But it can show you something other than the face — when it is distorted. When the mirror is not properly made. When the mirror is greasy or dirty. And let's say the mirror has got some curves on it — convexity, concavity — it is not going to show you your face as it is. And now, how much ever you try to change your face, still it looks different from what you want it to be. So you feel your face is too wide — then what should you do? Compress it? So the only way is to understand: this face has no problem. The problem is with this dumb mirror. And unless I recognize the problem belongs to the mirror, I'm always trying to go and find some other way to do it — to change something here.

So what you really love is yourself, and everything else is derived love. Because you think that you become okay when things around are okay. This idea has to be dismantled. The idea — the wrong idea — that everything else makes me happy, that everything else has what it takes to make me happy, to make me pleased, has to be dismantled. And dismantled at every level. Starting with the ones you love most — your family. Not that — please don't misunderstand this to say you should stop loving your family, okay? Don't have a fight at home, "I told you, you only love yourself, you don't love me." That's not the idea, okay? The idea is only to see clearly what is what. Alright. Nothing has to change on ground. It all has to change here. Okay. So with that background, let us enter back into the **vārtika**. So we saw a few of these verses last time. I will continue further now.

Correct. That's where I want to start. **na** **kasyāṃcidavasthāyām** **api|** **ātmā** **loke** **apriyo** **mataḥ|** **ātmā** means me, I. See, sometimes we say **ātmā** is dear — you may think **ātmā** is something else. No, we are talking about you. **kasyāṃcidavasthāyām|** In any situation whatsoever **ātmā** **na** **apriyo** **loke|** You are never a source of displeasure to yourself, ever. Why this statement? Because you think, I am the source of my own problems. I wasted my time for the last five years. I didn't do anything useful. I spent all my youth uselessly. Whatever you think, okay? A lot of guilt we have. So this is — even if you think so, and you say, okay, you know what, I'm going to jump off a cliff, because life is not worth it. Even if you do that — so that people think it's self-hate. That is not self-hate. That is self-love. Because the conclusions you have made, based on seeing the mind, is that with this mind, with this situation, with this body, with this life, I cannot be happy anymore. I want to be. See, I love myself — the pleased me. And if the pleased me is not there, I would rather end my life and get a fresh chance if possible — come back, you know, with another body. So I want to end my life. Why? Because in this life I can't make it. I can't make it. "I can make it. I had made it. I can't make it." All this is based on what? The understanding that you know what it means to be okay. Therefore he is saying: **kasyāṃcidavasthāyām** in any situation — even the worst situation, when you feel suicidal — even there, you think that this **duḥkha** which is there in the **ātmā**, you want to put an end to. You want to put an end to the sorrow centered on yourself. How do you do that? "I don't see another way. Put an end to this body." Even that is an indication of love for your pleased you.

He says, therefore, **loke** **apriyaḥ** **ātmā** is never true. It's always dear. **priyo'priyaśca** **jāyādiryathā'nātmā** **muhurmuhuḥ|** **anātmā** — whatever is other than I — is what? **muhurmuhuḥ** — again and again. What? **priyaḥ**, **apriyaḥ**, **priyaḥ**, **apriyaḥ|** Today — so nice, you are the best guy. Tomorrow — horrible fellow, why did you come to my life? Both are going on, okay? **muhurmuhuḥ** — repeatedly. One day smile, one day frown. So — not only about people, with respect to things also. You know — "I love you." "Why do you love me?" What should be the right answer? What will be the answer? Typically somebody asks, "Why do you love me? Tell me, why do you love me?" "Oh, you are so kind to me." Okay. Five days later, "I don't like you at all. I hate you." "Why? Why?" "You are never kind to me." Which is true? Which is true? Both of them are untrue. It is true at that time, for you. Okay? When the person gives in to your demands, she is very kind, or he is very kind. When they don't give in to your demand, they will be unkind. Which is true? Your demand is the problem, okay? The person is also doing what they can. But based on your demand, they respond differently. So what's the true answer? "Why do you love me?" "Because you please me." "Why do you not love me?" "Because you don't please me." That's the answer. Finally, that's the answer. But do you really please me, or displease me? No — you are also changing. Therefore pleasure and displeasure are changing. That's part of the story.

Suppose I am unchanged. You know — so now you have a situation. You have your house, or you bought a car, and the car you really love. Two days later, you don't love it. Did the car change? What changed? Your demands of the car changed. Are your demands ever fixed? They are ever-varying. Flavor of the day, flavor of the moment — they change. Is that you? Or do you know the change in demands? Therefore **śāstra** is being very objective. What do you really love? You really love a demand-free you. An agenda-free you. Is agenda a part of you? If it were a part of you, you cannot be free from it. What you can be free from is what is not inherent to you. That's the truth. Therefore, all the demands you have are extra baggage. And extra baggage you recognize as extra baggage — that's all. If you carry extra baggage, you have to pay for it. This is the rule, okay? For every kilo, you will pay. **bhagavān** allows a free allowance — zero free allowance, here. He says, you travel without any checked baggage. This is your hand baggage, okay? This body is your hand baggage — body, mind. You have to travel without any checked baggage. Any checked baggage, you have to pay. You decide — you decide whether you want to. "No, I need the baggage." You're going to go to a beautiful, you know, five-star hospitality, where everything is given to you. Why do you need to carry extra baggage? "No, no, I need extra baggage." Then you carry, and pay for it. So that idea has to be conveyed.

So what he is saying — he is making it clear that, you know, in no time are you displeased with yourself, or not dear to yourself. Whereas everything in the world is constantly a yo-yo, a seesaw. Like what? **jāyādiryathā|** So his example is what? This is dear, this is not dear — again, again. Like what? Like your own spouse. Now he is dear, now he is not dear. Now she is dear, now she is not dear. That's the truth.

**svata** **evāpriyo'nātmā** **hyātmaprītyarthasādhanāt|** **jāyādiḥ** **syātpriyo** **bhaktyā** **bandhakyāḥ** **kāmuko** **yathā|** So now he says— look, if **anātmā** is always **apriya**, generally **apriya**, **ātmā** alone is **priya**, then **anātmā** cannot make **ātmā** **priya**. You know, if it is **apriya**, it is **apriya** totally. So the answer is being given. **svata** **eva** **apriyaḥ** **anātmā|** **anātmā** by itself is always **apriya**. Always **apriya**, but when I like it, it is **priya**? No — it is always **apriya**. Then what is happening now? **ātmaprītyarthasādhanāt|** You yourself said what? The wife is dear for the pleased self, right? The husband is dear for the pleased self. Therefore it is a means to make me happy. The person, or the relation, or the thing, is a means to make me happy. Therefore it has some **prīti** in it, or not? It must have something in it. Otherwise, why does it make me happy? He says, it is **bhaktyā**. **bhakti** here is not devotion, okay? **bhakti** means **gauṇī**. **gauṇam** means secondary. It's not primary. Like you say, "Oh, this boy is like a lion." The lion-ness does not belong to the boy, really. You clearly see that there is bravery in the boy, and therefore you say **siṃho** **'yaṃ** **māṇavakaḥ**. It doesn't mean anything except that you are giving value based on something else — an attribute of the boy. But he is not a lion. So when you say **anātmā** is dear, it is purely figurative. Even when we say **anātmā** — wife is dear for the sake of the pleased self — wife is not dear even there. That is also purely **gauṇam** figurative. It's not real. That's what he is saying. So he gives an example, which is — you know, suppose there is a woman who lives by attracting men. Are those people really dear to her? Or is she really dear to them? It is all figurative.

nirhaitukī svataḥ prītirātmanyeva yatastataḥ| bhāktaṃ priyaṃ parityajya mukhyaṃ priyamupāśrayet॥ 83 ॥

**hetu** is a cause. **nirhetu** is what? **nirgatāḥ** **hetavaḥ** **yasmāt** — all causes have gone from which — that is **nirhetu**. **nirhetuka** is **nirhetu** only; **nirhaitukī** is feminine. So **svataḥ** **prītiḥ** **ātmanyeva** — the love you have for yourself is without reason. Why do you love yourself? Why are you pleased? "Oh, because my son got admission to a college." Why are you pleased? "Because my daughter is getting married." Forget it. Reason — there is no reason. If you think you have a reason to be happy, you will have the same reason to make you unhappy. You know — today, one year ago, this was such a wonderful day; today it all changed. One year has changed so much. Why? It all changed, I don't know — but if you are happy, why it all changed, it's good; if you are unhappy, it's a huge problem. Right? So he says — **nirhaitukī**. It's a very important thing. Why do you love a wife? Because she invokes the pleased self. Why do you love yourself? For no reason. Why are you happy? "Because everything is going well." Wrong answer. Incorrect means not analyzed. I am happy because happiness I am. There is no reason why I should be unhappy.

See — Swamiji would say, suppose you see a person who is laughing, or is smiling. Generally you don't go about asking, "Why are you happy? Why are you happy?" You generally don't go about asking — sometimes you do. But suppose that person has a long face, you can see that he or she has cried — do you ask, generally, or not? "What happened? Why are you sad? Why are you crying? What happened?" You don't ask this, generally — anybody you know well, if they are in tears, if they seem to have a long face, you ask, "What happened? What happened?" So the **vārtikakāra** is making it clear: **nirhaitukī** **svataḥ** **prītiḥ**. There is no reason why you love yourself. Because you are lovable. Love is your truth. Love means acceptance — self-acceptance. **ātmanyeva** **yataḥ** — since this love for yourself is natural, without any cause, uncaused — therefore, what should you do? **bhāktaṃ** **priyaṃ** **parityajya** **mukhyaṃ** **priyamupāśrayet|** Give up this object through which you are trying to derive that happiness. Okay — happiness is you. Now you are trying to derive it from something — from a dog, from a cat, from a tree, from a person. Don't do that. This is like a **vidhi**, you know. **bhāktaṃ** **priyam** — this figurative happiness which has — you give that up, and establish yourself in **mukhyaṃ** **priyam**, what is really dear to you. Why should I do so?

Whatever goes up will come down. **patanāntāḥ** **samucchrayāḥ|** **saṃyogā** **viprayogāntāḥ** — wherever there is a meeting, it ends in separation. Every meeting ends in separation. So what should you do? He is going to say again.

You want to enjoy it when it should become yours — it doesn't become. **asamprāpti** — you are yearning for it, you are thirsting for it; when it's not coming to you, what will happen? **duḥkhakṛt** — it will create sorrow. Then you got it. But even as you are very happy, and you know, you are coming home — and somebody picked your pocket. **evaṃ** **cet|** If this is so —**sādhanodbhūtaṃ** **yavat**-**kiñcit**-**sukham**-**matam** **tam** **niśeṣaṃ** **parityajya** if this is so, what should we do? What is absolute? That which is never subject to becoming **asukha**. Unconditional **sukha**. Somebody loves me conditionally; I love somebody conditionally; I love things conditionally. It means you have to know **tatpramā**. **darśanam** here is in terms of knowledge — should be known.

ātmabuddhiriyaṃ tāvatsarveṣāṃ jāyate svataḥ| aprāptā sarvamātmeti saivāto dhīrvidhīyate॥ 96 ॥

**ātmabuddhiriyaṃ** **tāvatsarveṣāṃ** **jāyate** **svataḥ** - This is **pūrvapakṣa**: - **ātmabuddhi** — then, you know, everybody knows **ātmā**, **anubhūtitaḥ**, it's experiential. Everybody knows, "I am this idiot." Who doesn't know? **jāyate** **svataḥ**. He says, we say — look, if you say "the color of the sky is blue," **veda** does not have to come and tell you the color of the sky is blue, okay? It won't. It has a lot more better business to do than come and tell you what you can know by yourself. Therefore, if **veda** has to come and tell you, it cannot be something that you figure out. So when you say "I know, I know myself, I know this is me" — that's how you know — that is not **pramā** — that is **bhrama**. What is **bhrama**? **bhrama** means not knowing it as it is — the mistake — is called **bhrama**. And until you see a snake, it is knowledge to you; until — "this is me," that is my knowledge — it is not **bhrama** right now. Only when you see something other than that as you, then alone this can be freed from the notion of being myself. Therefore **bhrama** will not — cannot — go, unless you have **pramā**. **pramā** means knowledge; **bhrama** means mistake — a thing which is mistaken, that mistake is not corrected unless you know what the thing really is. So when you say, "this knowledge of myself just comes naturally, it's so easy, everybody knows it" — no, sir, that is not what we are talking about. Because — yes, you know yourself, you are not saying you don't know yourself; you know yourself, but you don't know yourself as you are. What you know as yourself is indeed you. But you are not what you think you are. Mark this statement very carefully, okay. It looks like — what am I saying differently? I am saying, "Pot is clay." 100%. The pot says, "I am clay." Pot is clay. But clay is not just the pot. And you have to say, "I am clay," not just a pot. So here too — yes, this body, this **deha**, **sūkṣma**, **sthūla**, **kāraṇa** **śarīra** is all **ātmā** — but that is not all. That much is available to you, which is **ajñāna**, which is **apramā** — that is **bhrama**. Now what you need is **pramā**, **pramiti**: . That is **draṣṭavyaḥ**. And therefore, this **pramā** is what this **draṣṭavyaḥ** is going to present here.

You don't know that **ātmā** is all that is here. **sarvātmā**, **sarveṣām** **ātmā**, **sarvameva** **ātmā** — that you don't know. **saivāto** **dhīrvidhīyate** — that understanding is being sought to be brought to you, through the word **draṣṭavya** — **ātmā** **draṣṭavyaḥ**.
