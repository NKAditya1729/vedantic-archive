So, we were looking at the last mantra in the **Madhu Brāhmaṇa**. We'll go back to the text. So, **rūpaṃ rūpaṃ pratirūpo babhūva tadasya rūpaṃ praticakṣaṇāya**: to reveal its glory, its potential. So, to reveal its glory, etcetera — you know, to whom? The question was, you know, to whom will it reveal its glory? That's the way it is. I mean, it's all the way of presenting it.

But the fact is that we have a life, and we see a lot of things happening; we experience so many things. And if one is awakened to it, one is surprised as to how everything is, you know. So a Zen monk was apparently asked, you know, that he sees some miracles. "So what miracles have you seen?" He says, "You know, I eat food: it's a miracle." So you look at it: you know, I walk, I talk, the sun rises, the sun sets every day, the seasons go around. It's a miracle. It is a miracle, in a way, because what puts all these in motion? And there is only one, and it appears as a seer, as a seen, as a thinker, thought. What more miracle do you want? But that fellow said, "That's not — I am expecting a miracle. This is not a miracle." Hey, it is a miracle.

So if you look at it that way — I am saying, if you have that — because we take so many things for granted, you know. As you grow older, you realise what you took for granted when you were younger, but it is too late to go back in time. You wasted so much of your time and energy doing things which were unproductive. You fought causes which were not worth fighting for, and you didn't fight causes which are worth fighting for. I mean, whatever. All I am saying is: you only in hindsight, you know, what perhaps could have been done differently. And that is also not necessarily right, that you should have done it differently, because if you did it differently, you wouldn't arrive at where you are today. So everything in life has, you know, some purpose which we don't understand. We don't understand why, because there is only one thing that could be understood. That's the way. So I am just saying, understand: **praticakṣaṇāya** is to be understood that way. **indro māyābhiḥ pururūpa īyate**.

**indraḥ parameśvaraḥ māyābhiḥ prajñābhiḥ nāmarūpabhūtakṛtamithyābhimānairvā na tu paramārthataḥ** — not. It's not really there are many. It is not really **mahimā**, or **mahimā** — a separate fellow who doesn't have **mahimā**, etcetera. All of this is just an appearance. So how does he go about doing this?

So he says: suppose, you know, you imagine you have a lot to show. Like, modern day, I would say, you know, you have a city or a national park, or you have something which wants to showcase itself. How would it showcase itself? There are different aspects to it. So you may have a situation where you have a mountain and ocean both together; in California it is like that. So then you have to showcase both. So you might need to do different things. One of the hiking trips will be there; then, you know, a deep-diving trip perhaps. I mean, whatever it takes, okay? You have whale watching, dolphin watching; there are so many things going on. Now each of them requires a different way of going about it, because, same way, you can't experience both. So this world is so manifold.

It says, you know, it's like the — there are horses yoked to chariots. And what is the purpose of these horses and chariots? They are meant to take the person in the chariot all over the place, wherever he wants to go. It's a good tourism, you know: **dekho dekho**. See all the things you want to see; you watch them all. So how do you do that? So there must be **karaṇa**s to do it. And therefore, **harayaḥ**: **yuktāḥ harayaḥ**.

**yuktāḥ ratha iva vājinaḥ** — **rathe iva vājinaḥ**. Not **rathaḥ iva**: **rathe iva vājinaḥ**. Like to a chariot you have yoked horses. And what are they meant for? So, **viṣayaprakāśane**. And they will take the person who is, you know, sitting. So even nowadays still there is a fancy for a horse carriage. You just don't go in a car; you sit in a horse carriage and you see the city. So the horses take you around; they take you all around. So what is the chariot here? **śarīrameva rathaḥ**. **indriyāṇi hayāḥ**. The **indriya**s are the **haya**s. For whom are they meant? For the **rathin**, the person who is sitting in the chariot: the **rathasvāmī** — not even **rathin**, **rathasvāmī**. And you may have a charioteer, or a driver also, who knows the place well, how to lead you there. Nowadays you have what? You have a driverless car. A driver is there; all the AI is sitting in there. Some driver only is driving the driverless car.

So — but the fact is, all this is meant for whom? For the rider. Who is that rider? So you enjoy your ride. Then who made you enjoy the ride? That is **indro māyābhiḥ pururūpam īyate**. You imagine, if you lost one sense organ, what would your world view be? No eyes: same world continues; what is your world view? Lost your smell; lost your taste. Each one will strip off one **mahimā** of life, one glory. So that — all that. And why do we have so many names and forms? Because there are so many sights to be seen, and for each of them you need different instruments. Therefore, in each living being the **indriya**s are of a different kind, and there are so many different kinds of **indriya**s, you know, as many as the living beings themselves. And they all have their own **viṣaya**s, you know. On account of this you have, you know, so many. Therefore he says, **asya harayaḥ śatā**, **daśa ca**: 100 plus 10 plus 1000, etcetera. All these are meant for what? **anantāni**.

"Oh, so they are all different from me?" No. **ayaṃ vai harayaḥ**: these horses are indeed the same reality alone. So there is no real difference.

**prāṇibhedasya ānantyāt** — **prāṇibhedānantyāt**: this should have been a compound. **prāṇibhedānantyāt**. Okay.

**kiṃ bahunā ?** What more can we say? **tadetadbrahma** — this **brahma** is what? **ya ātmā**: it is me. What is it? **apūrvam**. **apūrvam**.

**nāsya kāraṇaṃ pūrvaṃ vidyata iti apūrvam** — **pūrvam** is what? **kāraṇam**. So sometimes **bhāṣyakāra** will put the word before. He did not say **pūrvaṃ kāraṇam**; he said **kāraṇaṃ pūrvam**. Both are possible. So for this Brahman there is no predecessor. Okay: this came from that, that came from that; the Brahman came from what? **aisā nahīṃ hai**. Brahman didn't come from anything: **apūrvam**. "Oh, Brahman did not come from anything; from that everything came. From Brahman everything came — correct or not? **jagatkāraṇaṃ brahma**: **jagat** came from Brahman or not?" Look: whether Brahman is born, or something is born of Brahman — if something is really born of Brahman, if there is real birth, then Brahman is changing. If Brahman itself is born, it is in time; it is changing. So, either way, there is no **jāti**. **ajātiḥ eva satyam**. There is no change; there is no birth. It's all, you know — it is not a real **pariṇāmi upādānam** at all. What is Brahman? **vivarta upādānam**. Without changing, without undergoing any change, it becomes, as though, many. That's why the example is a rope-snake: without being touched, the rope becomes the snake. So these words here are telling us whatever we said earlier, **jagatkāraṇaṃ brahma**, etcetera. Now you understand what it is all about. **apūrvam**: it doesn't have a cause. Then what is next after that? **anaparam**. **apūrvam**, **anaparam**.

**nāsyāparaṃ kāryaṃ vidyata iti anaparam** — this should have been in blues. Okay. So, **nāsyāparaṃ kāryaṃ vidyate**, which means what? There is no effect. There is no cause for Brahman; there is no effect for Brahman. Which means what? Brahman is Brahman all the time; it never became anything.

**nāsya jātyantaramantarāle vidyata iti anantaram** — one more word. So there is nothing in between also: **antarāle madhye api na vidyate**. **anantaram**.

**tathā bahirasya na vidyata iti abāhyam** — **abāhyam**: there is nothing outside it. When you say nothing outside it: nothing which is different from it, which is outside. Nothing — that is, nothing which is independently different. "Oh, I see so many things outside." Yes, they are there. But whatever you see, starting with your body onwards, your mind onwards, your **ahaṅkāra** onwards — **ahaṅkārādidehāntam** — whatever you see, all of them, and the **viṣaya**s, they all do not have a second being. Then what is being? That being — that being is I, the I. Or take it as Brahman. See, basically the point: I will say "I", but the fact is, we should arrive at it. So I am telling you: Brahman is all these. It is not a **kārya**; it's not a **kāraṇa**; it's not outside; it's not inside. Then you have to arrive at what — what is the solution to this jigsaw puzzle. They are giving you all the pieces. If I tell you the solution, it is not a solution; the solution, I have to arrive at. And in that arrival there is, you know — he says what? **abāhyam**: it is not outside.

**kiṃ punastat nirantaraṃ brahma ?** It is **nirantaram**. Okay. It is **nirantaram**, because it has nothing in between also. There is no gap. It's **ghanaḥ**. See, when you say **nāsya jātyantaramantarāle vidyate**, there is nothing else. So there is Brahman, then there is some space, then there is some more — so it's split Brahman? No, there is nothing else. Therefore **Śaṅkarācārya** says, **kiṃ punastat nirantaram**: there is no interval, there is no gap; there is only one. **kiṃ punaḥ**? What is that?

**ayamātmā** — now **śruti** says, that is **ayamātmā**. Every human being's perception is — every perception is one experience. Every experience is distinct. Life is a series of experiences, a series of knowledge taking place, because each experience is really knowledge. There is no — there is no experience without knowledge. Experience is knowledge of experience. This word "experience" sometimes is a problem, because you say, "You have to experience sugar." Sugar is not knowledge? "Do you know sugar? I will explain to you: white crystals." That is knowledge. Do you understand what sugar is? You don't. So you need to experience sugar. The only way to know sugar, because you are talking of taste, is to taste it. If you are talking of smell — oh, you know, you see a rose. Do you know what a rose means just because it's white? You don't. You have to experience it. Means what? You have to know it. You have to know it in terms of the smell, that's all. You have to know it.

Now, any experience: how do you experience anything? How do you experience anything? Who experiences? You have to say "I experience", because you are the fellow, the experiencer. Who is that you, without whom there is no experience? That you is **ayamātmā**.

**ko'sau ?** Who is this **ātmā**? **bhāṣyakāra** is being very kind. **ko'sau ?** Who is this **ātmā**?

**yaḥ pratyagātmā** — **pratyak** means what? **āntaraḥ**. How **āntaraḥ**? **na bāhyaḥ**. See, **abāhyam**. **abāhyam** means that which is never seen as outside; means that which never becomes **dṛṣṭigocara**; which means that which never comes to be known through a process of knowing; that which never comes to being through a process of becoming. That is **ātmā**. Everything you experience comes to be experienced, and that experience goes. No experience is forever. Even when you feel it's forever — you are sitting on a plane, and that plane's constant drone is there till you land — but still it is not forever. The fact that you could recognise it as a drone means you have had life without that drone. The fact that I can understand "I want to be different" means I have been different. **saṃsāra** is not possible unless there is already an understanding of freedom. Except that the freedom came in moments, and I associated that freedom with something else.

And now what is being said is: it is **nirantaram**; there is no interval in this. And that **nirantaram** is who? **pratyak**. **pratyak** — the translation typically is "inner" or "innermost". That "innermost" takes us to a spatial frame, generally. So you want to now see inside; you go inside, deeper in the heart. Expressions are okay; any expression is all right. But that expression should not mislead, you know; it should not become something spatial. See, that is why there are people who will say **ātmā** is **aṇu**: it's tinier, very tiny, so tiny you will go and find it somewhere inside. Not true. "Inner" means that which never becomes outer: can never be externalised, can never be objectified. That is why it is **sarvāntaraḥ**. **nirantaraḥ**: it's everywhere. **sarvāntaraḥ** — you know, **nirantaraḥ**: no gap; **sarvāntaraḥ**: it's everywhere. No place where it is not.

So, **ayamātmā**, **pratyagātmā**. Who is he? **draṣṭā**: you are seeing all these places, **dekho dekho**; you are seeing everything; that fellow is you. **śrotā**: you are hearing these words; that fellow is you. **mantā**: the fellow thinking is you. **boddhā**: one who knows, one who has woken up, is you. **vijñātā**. All of these are you. "Then I am **saṃsārī**?" No. You are all of these, yet you are free from all of them. That's your **mahimā**. So, **sarvānubhūḥ**. **sarvānubhūḥ**. What do you mean by **sarvānubhūḥ**?

**sarvātmanā sarvamanubhavati** — one who — **bhāṣyakāra**: **sarvātmanā sarvamanubhavati**. Being the very being of everything: **sarvātmanā**. Experiences everything. Being everything: **sat**. Experiencing everything: **cit**. There is no **sat** different from **cit**: **sadeva cit**, **cideva sat**. Therefore what are you? **sarvānubhūḥ**. You are the experiencer; you are the experienced. You are the thinker; you are the thought; you are the thinking. Seeing: I call myself a seer. Hearing: you just look at it, right? I have been hearing; I am a hearer. Hearing goes: am I gone? I am seeing; sight stops: am I gone? I am tasting something very delicious, ice cream. I am tasting. Ice cream — how long does it stay? That's why ice cream: it melts away. Then what are you now? I was a taster. That's with reference to taste: I was a taster. Without reference to taste, without reference to hearing, without reference to sight, without reference to any movement, any action — but every action you are. Therefore, **sarvānubhūḥ**.

You are the one who is the truth of the experience; you are the truth of the experiencer. You are the content of the experiencer — if I say that, you think experience is outside. So again you have to correct that. It is the truth of experiencer and experienced. But experience outside — that's until you locate yourself as the experiencer. You cannot separate an experience from the experiencer or the experienced. There is no hearing unless there is a hearer and a heard; you cannot have hearing without them. One phenomenon alone is divided — divided. And that's how **vyavahāra** is possible: one thing at a time. That's how we are; the bodies, minds are designed that way. And why did he do it that way? So that you can experience the glory of the truth. That's how it's presented.

So, **vijñātā sarvānubhūḥ**: **sarvātmanā sarvamanubhavati**: **sarvānubhūḥ**. **aura kyā**, what else should I say? **ityetadanuśāsanam**. I don't know why, in this particular verse, this time we have so many of things — all these are missing. The **pratīka**s are all a little messed up here. Okay. So just keep in mind: I could have used it, but I wasn't using it; I have my document. So it is, you know: **ayamātmā** — **ityetadanuśāsanam**.

**ityetadanuśāsanam sarvavedāntopadeśaḥ** — **ityetadanuśāsanam** means what? **sarvavedāntopadeśaḥ**. It's not — **bhāṣyakāra** here is saying: it's not just "I am concluding the **Madhu Brāhmaṇa**"; it is not that "I am concluding the second chapter"; it is not "I am concluding the **Madhu Kāṇḍa**", which is the first and second chapters of **Bṛhadāraṇyaka**. It is **sarvavedāntopadeśaḥ**: all **Vedānta**, only this much they have to say. You go one more step, you can say **sarvavedopadeśaḥ**: nothing more.

**eṣa sarvavedāntānāmupasaṃhṛto'rthaḥ** — why did you say that, **sarvavedāntopadeśaḥ**? Because **eṣa sarvavedāntānāmupasaṃhṛto'rthaḥ**. This is the conclusion; this is what you arrive at through every **vedāntavākya**. Every **upaniṣad** has only this one, you know: **tattu samanvayāt**, the fourth **sūtra**. All of them have **samanvaya** only in this one truth. What is this?

**etadamṛtamabhayam** — **parisamāptaśca śāstrārthaḥ**. This is the one which is timeless; this is the one which frees you from insecurity of any kind, this knowledge. **śāstra** is over: **parisamāptaḥ**, **paritaḥ samāptaḥ**. There is nothing more to be said; it is done. That's how the **Madhu Kāṇḍa** concludes. Very, very nice. So you can see, finally, **ityetadanuśāsanam**. **ityetadanuśāsanam** means the idea is, there are no more things to be said, because it is **sarvāntaraḥ**, **sarvānubhūḥ**, **nirantaraḥ**. What else can you talk about?

So — unless I do not understand Brahman — there is no second thing. If I don't understand Brahman, there are many; definitely there are many. It's not one; subject–object will always be there. But if the subject–object oneness is understood, then how do you count a second thing? And there is nothing to be counted, and you understand all that is here is me, without a process of knowing, without a process of becoming. Then what else is to be done? To say, **hā~**, okay, now what? Means I haven't understood. Correct? All there is: I understand, but there is something which makes me come back to my old ways of thinking again and again. I may need to get to that clarity, because that is why this **saṃśaya** has to go. Doubt has to go. Then the contradictory feeling has to go — has to go, from the perspective of the one who is struggling with it. But for one who has understood, what is there?

"No, Swamiji, but when in the class you tell me, I correct myself; but again, when I go to the world, I get lost. What is this going on? What is all this happening?" You know, that kind of a question can arise. It's not something that can be denied, that a question like that arises. So, you know, somebody had a question. Question is what? You know, it's like: why listening and understanding doesn't persist? You know: "If I listen to the class, then I feel that, you know, I understood. When I go away, again it goes away." It's true; it's how it feels. But examine what went and what came. You are — see, you are that person who says "experience went away" and "experience came", which means you are looking at freedom as something to be experienced for some time. It's an experience; it's still in time. For that to change, I have to see: the experiencer is me. I am not an experiencer. That "I" has to arrive — really arrive — at the one who always is. It's not that I go; it's not that I come; it's not that I sleep; it's not that I wake up. I do all this, but I am unchanging.

So the fact is, anything — any one moment, any one change — I can talk about only because I am there. **anvaya**: **anvaya** is **aham asmi**. **vyatireka** is everything that can go. Everything else goes: **avasthā**s go, **śarīra**s go. **aham** never goes, because to report one thing in the universe I need to be there. So if I am able to appreciate that presence as me, without having to go through a process of thinking — see, if I ask myself "Who am I?", or if I ask you "Who are you?", do you think to say "I am Devadatta"? How long does it take to say "I am Devadatta", **asmi**? And how well entrenched is it for you to say it, even if you are woken up in the middle of the night? You will say it. I don't think anybody will say, "Oh, let me think who I am." I don't think it happens. Only in the **Vedānta** class they think, try to think who we are. Otherwise, who I am is very clear to me: Devadatta. With that identity I want to become free. Devadatta can never become free; Devadatta's freedom will only come and go. That Devadatta has to know that: **devadattanāmā aham asmi**. That's just a name given to this expression. But what is only that existence, by which alone everything is coming to light?

I count many because I identify with the mind and body. So the question is, you know: if I don't have this clarity, then what should I do? Then we will say, **nididhyāsanam**: **śravaṇaṃ mananaṃ nididhyāsanam**. But the fact is, when is **jñānam** really there? One can say one has **jñānam** and one is trying to gain **niṣṭhā**: **jñānaniṣṭhā**. But when there is only one thing, where is **niṣṭhā**? But based on the human experience we have all these words, because the teacher also understands, the student also understands, the difficulty. And both have to say: finally there is no **dvaitam**, but still we feel that way. So it's a very funny world, you know; it's a very funny business. But if I don't have to become anything different, then **etāvadanuśāsanam**. Because what is — I am okay with the body, with this given mind, with the world, with all its headaches. Nothing needs to change, because I see that all these are my glories. All the movements are my glory. Nothing is really happening. That understanding is what — that is what is called **niścayajñānam**.

So that knowledge is such that it is not a knowledge in the normal parlance that we use. It's not a knowledge that can be forgotten. Because suppose you say, "I forgot something" — **mattaḥ smṛtirjñānamapohanaṃ ca** — the one who says "I forgot" is you, not what you forgot. The one who says "I have an experience of forgetting" — he is **sarvānubhūḥ**. So the experience of forgetting is you; you are the fellow who experienced forgetting. "I forgot, Swamiji; the **Vedānta** words I forgot." You are there. How can you forget? "I remembered." You are there. How can you remember? You are always there. It's not something you can remember or forget. What can be remembered is what can be forgotten. So, until I am thinking there is something I have to remember, it means it is a concept. It is a concept. Any concept I may forget, because anything which is gathered is subject to going. Therefore, thinking that I can forget just shows that I haven't understood, finally. What is subject to being experienced is not me. **sarvānubhūḥ**: what is available in every experience. **pratibodhaviditam**. There is no experience where it is not. Recognising that alone is understanding.

And if we really understand that clearly, then — **kimicchankasya kāmāya śarīramanusaṃjvaret** — for what reason would we do anything? Everything would be okay; whatever happens will be okay. You still continue to do; you don't need to go into some, you know, state of non-doing. **prārabdha** takes each person's life differently. Somebody may be sitting in **samādhi** for his whole life, and somebody force-feeds him; that's his **prārabdha**. It doesn't necessarily mean that he is more, you know, knowledgeable; it doesn't mean that. So — "Swamiji, there are seven levels." You want to go for seven levels? "I should go to the **saptama**", you know — that seventh level — is **saṃsāra**, is it not? "No, no: only then I won't come back." Who knows whether you will come back, or what will come back? Right now you are not saying you are going, coming. What will come back? If you are not born, where is the question of rebirth? But that level of conviction in my understanding, which is not mistaken, which cannot be improved upon — that is **etāvadanuśāsanam**. There is nothing more to be said.

Now what remains is to remove what prevents me from appreciating this. And what prevents me from appreciating it is my own, you know — of course, **avidyā**; that's all the problem really is. But what prevents removing **avidyā** when it's told? I am holding on to some things; I want to hold on; and I hold a certain identity. You know, I think that certain things are fair and unfair, and I am very vocal about it; I can't but do something about it. It's fine; nothing wrong; everything is fine in its own place. But understand that, until "I think this is not right, that is right, this is wrong" softens, we have to continue to struggle. Because when you cannot accept something — you cannot hide in a forest forever, okay? And even if you hide, if you come out of **samādhi**, you will remember all the unfairness of the world. It is not going to go away. This is why it is very important to appreciate that what is, is in order in its own way. I am not saying it is right or it is wrong; please understand that.

Right and wrong really is here, because when everything is Brahman, what is wrong? With respect to **dharma** we will say — because what will help you more towards this truth is to be kept. So it doesn't mean — you know, we will say, for a person who is a **jñānī**, even if he kills, no **pāpa**. What does it mean? He will not kill; that's a fact.

So then, what prevents my owning this is that I still have my agenda for the world. I have an agenda for this body; I have an agenda for the mind. Which means I am still identified with the mind, 100%. It's a quieter mind, perhaps; it's a well-informed mind, correct. But I need to know that I don't call the shots. And even the mind which is quiet for days — one fine day it just goes into riot, and you know that you were okay because you were identified as a quiet mind. Then only you realise that it's a quiet mind which you took as "I am free". Now you are not free; it just took the right kind of disturbance to get you out. So I am saying, I don't have to really do anything, but I have to do something. Why do I have to do something? I do something so that I have dealt with all these difficulties. And disturbances come in life, because you don't know when they will come; they can come any moment. Just because I went to heaven, let us say, and spent, you know, 10 million years there — when I come back, I will again have a problem, right?

So many a time people feel that way, you know: you are living in a very nice, settled place; everything looks good; now you feel "I am totally okay". But some event can shake me up. So, if that event shouldn't shake me up, I should never have that sense of well-being which is dependent upon my mind being calm, or the body being okay. I have to examine what makes me comfortable. Generally, I am saying, even as we grow, generally what makes us comfortable is: the mind is calm; it's not demanding that anything should be different. As soon as a thought comes, "This is not okay; this has to go; this has to change" — then there is no — unless that "it should change, it should go" is optional. So you say, you know, "There should be trees around. There are no trees; everybody is cutting trees." All right, I'll go and plant five trees. But if somebody cuts, they cut; that's how it is. I can only plant. The freedom to plant remains mine, but the freedom to keep every tree I planted is not mine. I plant; but that the plant dies is also not in my hand.

So basically I am saying, all action has to be born of that freedom. If the action is for the sake of making the world a better place, or making myself a better person, then that action is still within the realm of growing, becoming. Nothing wrong with the effort. So then, what is it? The only thing that frees me is the understanding. That is why we say **jñānādeva kaivalyam**: no other way. But for that **jñāna**, for that **jñānaniṣṭhā**, whatever needs to be done to remove the **pratibandhaka** may require some action, because growing into any attitude requires an action. So the more compelled I feel to act, the stronger are my compulsions to act, or compulsions to avoid. That is **rāgadveṣa**. I can justify it: "No, no, I cannot but do it." That justification doesn't do me any good, finally. It may be in a good way: "This society is not being fair." Maybe. But if you feel very strongly, it means the society is real, its actions are real, your mind is real, everything is real. In that real world, **saṃsāra** will be real, 100%. So it doesn't mean — if you accept all the inequities in the world — did you create the world? Do you — are you in charge? Try, try taking charge; you will be destroyed one day, too soon. You can't take charge. "But I can make it —" Yeah, you can make it a better place, 100%. You can help a million people. Good, wonderful, do it. I am not against social, you know, causes, working for them. Please understand. Anything I say is misinterpreted; that's unfortunate at times, you know. Understandable; that's how their mind works. But that doesn't make it right. And "what I think alone is right, and all other things are wrong" is wrong. One has to have that openness as to what is being said, in what context, to whom, where and when, and why. Suppose somebody says, "I want to do something", and you say "Do it" when it's not the best thing for the person — why should they do it? One has to think about it at times. You know, there is a lot of thinking in terms of saying, "Among **sādhana**s, this alone will do." But it's not necessarily true. So I am saying, in terms of **sādhana**s, each person is unique, and each person's circumstances are unique. But **etāvaddhi anuśāsanam**: there is nothing more to be said. If this is understood, this is all. "See, Swamiji, if this is not understood?" Then start again. Start again what? Read the **Maitreyī** **Brāhmaṇa** again. Read **Ajātaśatru** again. Read the **Madhu Brāhmaṇa**, till it is understood. **pavanapuṇyena śravaṇaṃ kuryāt**. Gain **adhikāritvam**. "So **adhikāritvam**, Swamiji, is real? It is other than **advaitam**?" No, it's not. **adhikāritvam** is also part of the **dvaita**. But through **dvaita** only you walk up to **advaita**, because that's human life. Right. So, **etāvaddhi anuśāsanam**. A lineage, okay? A lineage. A lineage of what? A lineage of teachers. And what did they do? Each one communicated the uncommunicable to the next person, without any loss. That's what is communicated. It is not the ways of doing which are always communicated; that's part of it. See, **prakriyā**s are ways of doing things. But what really needs to get communicated is that vision: that there is only one reality, despite all the apparent differences. And that vision really is what has to come in the **vaṃśa**. Not just saying, you know, "My father did this; you know, they celebrated Pongal, so I should also celebrate Pongal." That's okay, because the form and the spirit go hand in hand. Without any forms, the spirit will die; without spirit, the form will die. People don't appreciate this. This is something that **Pūjya** Swami used to highlight at times, you know: that the form and the spirit, both are needed.
